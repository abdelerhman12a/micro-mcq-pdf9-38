Protozoa
Protozoa are unicellular organisms that occur singly or in colony formation.
● Each protozoan is a complete unit capable of performing all functions.
● Protozoa have a wide range of sizes (1-150µ).
● The structure of the protozoan cell is formed of a cytoplasmic body and a nucleus.
Rhizopoda
These are a group of protozoa that move by pseudopodia.
Entamoeba histolytica
● E.histolytica has a worldwide distribution, especially in tropical developing
countries and low socio-economic communities. It secretes histolytic secretions
which destroy human cells.
Life cycle and transmission:
- Hosts: Man is the only host for E. histolytica, and there are no animal reservoirs.
- Habitat: Large intestine especially caecum and sigmoido-rectal colon. The
parasite sometimes inhabits extra-intestinal sites like the liver, pleura, pericardium,
brain, and skin.
- Diagnostic stage: cyst or trophozoite.
- Infective stage: The quadri-nucleated cyst.
- Mode(s) of infection:
1. Man gets infected by swallowing the cyst in contaminated food or water.
2. External autoinfection (through anal-hand infection) may occur.
3. Mechanical transmission by flies and cockroaches.

5. Use of human feces as fertilizers.
Clinical picture
I- Intestinal manifestations:
a- Asymptomatic cases passing cysts in
feces.
b- Symptomatic non dysenteric
amoebiasis: The patient presents diarrhea, Life cycle of Entamoeba
with abdominal colic and nausea. The
stools may contain mucous but no blood.
c- Acute amoebic dysentery: There’s dysentery which means tenesmus, and
frequent motions of loose stools with blood and mucous.
N.B. The early lesion is a small area of necrosis or ulcer characterized by raised
edges but little or no inflammation between the lesions.
Trophozoites spread in the submucosa and kill host cells as they progress, resulting
in the classic flask-shaped ulcer with a wide base and undermined edge. Ulcer
expansion can penetrate the serous layer leading to perforation of the intestinal
wall. This perforation may cause local abscesses or generalized peritonitis.
In amoebiasis, intestinal epithelial cells are killed due to direct contact with the
parasite leading to disruption of the intestinal mucosa. Adherence, cytotoxicity,
and disintegration of tissues are other important factors in the pathogenesis of E.
histolytica. This is mediated by specific parasite proteins, including mainly
photolytic enzymes.
d- Fulminating amoebic colitis: Severe rapidly characterized by frequent

e- Amoeboma: Painful abdominal mass which frequently occurs in caecum and
descending colon which may be mistaken as tumor.
N.B. Does the trophozoite invade the intestinal wall?
Yes, that's why there’s blood in stools, which is the major cause of E.histolytica
pathogenicity.
N.B. What is the fate of the trophozoite in the colon?
Trophozoite has one of three ways:
1. It remains in the lumen, then pass in stools as cyst stage.
2. It invades the mucosa leading to inflammation and ulcer.
3. It invades the submucosa, passes through blood to the extra-intestinal sites like
liver, lung, brain and skin.
N.B. Amoebic dysentery should be differentiated from bacillary dysentery
caused by the gram negative bacilli Schigella. The following characters are
present in bacillary dysentery:
1. Sudden onset of symptoms with more severe general manifestations.
2. It often occurs in epidemics.
3. By sigmoidoscopy, the ulcers are serpiginous with severe inflammation of
the tissues between them.
4. Stools is less offensive, watery, alkaline, contains many macrophages and
leucocytes, with no cysts or trophozoites.
II- Extra- Intestinal manifestations:
a- Amoebic hepatitis and abscess: Dissemination of primary infection occurs via the
bloodstream. The liver is the most commonly affected organ. Initially, the lesions
are small foci of necrosis forming a single large abscess. The center of the abscess
is formed of chocolate-colored necrotic tissues, consisting of lysed hepatocytes,
erythrocytes, bile, and fat. The patient presents with hepatomegaly, liver pain, and
tenderness with fever and anorexia.

b- Pulmonary amoebiasis: Hematogenous spread of trophozoites to other sites such
as lung occurs. Pulmonary infection usually results from direct extension of
hepatic lesion across the diaphragm. It is characterized by chest pain, fever, cough,
and dyspnea. The sputum may contain trophozoites.
c- Infection of other organs (e.g., brain, spleen, pericardium) is rare with clinical
symptoms related to the affected organ.
d- Cutaneous amoebiasis is a complication of intestinal amoebiasis. It may occur in
the perianal region, or may appear in the skin surrounding a fistula in case of
abscesses involving the abdominal wall. It appears as a wet granular necrotic ulcer
with prominent borders.
Diagnosis:
1. Detection of cyst or trophozoite in stools samples. The cysts appear in formed
stools, while trophozoites in the diarrheic stools. In amoebic dysentery, stools is
offensive, acidic, has many red blood cells, few macrophages, and leucocytes.
Fresh stool may be examined immediately for motile trophozoites. Concentration
stool techniques are used to detect cysts. Stained stool specimens with iodine,
eosin, or trichrome help in the visualization of the parasite. The culture of stools on
a special media is difficult but very sensitive.
2. Serological diagnosis: ELISA uses monoclonal antibodies to detect the amoebic
antigen (coproantigen) in the stools.
N.B. Serological methods to detect antibodies are used only for epidemiological
surveys as these antibodies persist for years in the serum after cure. ELISA, IFAT,
IHAT, and counterimmunoelectrophoresis are the commonly used tests.
3. Sigmoidoscopy to demonstrate characteristic lesions (flask shape ulcer) and take
aspirate or biopsy from these lesions.

4. Hepatic lesions are diagnosed by imaging methods like ultrasonography,
computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Aspiration of
hepatic fluid to detect trophozoites may be needed.
5. In Pulmonary amoebiasis, examination of sputum may detect trophozoites.
N.B. E.histolytica should be differentiated from E.dispar (non-pathogenic
morphologically identical species) by isoenzyme analysis, monoclonal antibodies
and DNA technology.
Treatment:
a- Asymptomatic cyst passers: luminal drugs (drugs which affect stage in the lumen
of the intestine) are used. These include: iodoquinol, diloxanide furoate, or
paromomycin.
b- Symptomatic amoebiasis should be treated with tissue drugs which affect
invasive stages whether in liver or other tissues. These include: metronidazole
(flagyl) or tinidazole (fasigyn).
c- Drainage of liver abscess with needle aspiration or surgical drainage, is only
indicated in large abscesses with a high probability of rupture.
Prevention and Control:
- Personal cleanliness and Hygiene.
- Careful washing of fruits and vegetables.
- Safe water supply.
- Sanitary disposal of human feces.
- Treatment of both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients to reduce
transmission.

Acanthamoeba keratitis
Causative agent: Acanthamoeba keratitis, or AK, is a rare but serious infection
of the eye that can cause permanent vision loss or blindness . This infection is
caused by a tiny ameba (single-celled living organism) called Acanthamoeba.
Transmission:
Infection of the eye occurs when the Acanthamoeba organisms contained in water
or contact lens solution enter the eye through small scrapes that can be caused by
contact lens wear or other minor eye injuries.
For people who wear contact lenses, certain practices can increase the risk of
getting Acanthamoeba keratitis:
1. Storing and handling lenses improperly.
2. Disinfecting lenses improperly (such as using tap water or homemade
solutions to clean the lenses).
3. Swimming, using a hot tub, or showering while wearing lenses.
Pathogenesis:
Many studies have revealed that a major virulence protein of Acanthamoeba is a
mannose-binding protein that mediates the adhesion of amoebae to the surface of
the cornea and also plays a role in events that occur subsequent to the adhesion and
lead to the development of a devastating cytopathic effect.
Clinical manifestations:
Symptoms of AK include:
● Sensation of something in the eye.
● Eye pain
● Eye redness

Sleeping while wearing contact lenses has been shown to cause up to 8 times
greater risk of an eye infection
2. Wash Your Hands:
Always wash your hands with soap and water before handling your lenses.
Dry your hands well with a clean cloth before touching your contact lenses every
time.
3. Keep Contact Lenses Away from All Water:
Water can introduce germs to the eyes through contact lenses. Remove contact
lenses before swimming and avoid showering in them.
