1. The parasite and its hosts

PointWhat the book says
Name and diseaseToxoplasma gondii, disease toxoplasmosis. An obligate intracellular protozoan that can infect all types of cells, mainly reticuloendothelial cells, all over the body.
Definitive hostThe cat: the sexual stage is completed in the gut of the cat. Only members of the cat family shed oocysts in stool.
Intermediate hostsMan, mammals (rat, cattle, any herbivorous animal) and birds. They harbour the parasite in any nucleated cell.
Who gets itMost human infections are asymptomatic, but it is a common opportunistic infection in HIV/AIDS and in organ transplant patients. Infection is highest in hot, humid climates at lower altitudes because oocysts survive better there.

2. The three forms (morphology)

FormFeatures
Oocyst11-14 x 9-11 um. Contains two sporocysts, each with 4 sporozoites (disporocystic tetrazoic). Shed only by cats.
TrophozoiteObligate intracellular, crescent-shaped, 6 x 2 um, pointed anteriorly and rounded posteriorly, multiplies by binary fission. Two types: tachyzoite (actively proliferating, acute stage) and bradyzoite (slowly dividing, chronic cases).
Tissue cystMost common in brain, skeletal and cardiac muscle, but can occur in any organ. Contains hundreds of slowly growing bradyzoites.
PseudocystNo cyst wall. Formed in many nucleated cells. Contains 50-100 rapidly growing tachyzoites.

Diagnostic stage: cysts and pseudocysts in tissues. Infective stage: all life forms are infectious to the intermediate host.

3. Life cycle in one pass

  • The cat is infected by ingesting oocysts from faecal contamination, or tissue cysts in the flesh of animals it eats.
  • Excystation in the small intestine; trophozoites penetrate sub-mucosal epithelial cells and undergo schizogony and gametogony, giving microgametocytes (male) and macrogametocytes (female).
  • Fertilised macrogametocytes become oocysts, discharged into the gut lumen and excreted. Oocysts sporulate in the warm environment and become infectious.
  • Man and farm animals ingest sporulated oocysts from food contaminated with cat stool. Sporozoites penetrate the intestinal mucosa and reach macrophages, where they divide rapidly (tachyzoites) and form a cyst that may occupy the whole cell.
  • Infected cells rupture, releasing tachyzoites that enter other cells including muscle and nerve cells, where they are protected from the immune system and multiply slowly as bradyzoites.
  • Cysts in man and farm animal tissues are infectious to cats and other carnivorous animals, including man.

4. How humans get infected

  • Congenital: transplacental, only when a woman becomes infected during pregnancy. First-trimester infection is more severe.
  • Postnatal: food contaminated with oocysts from cat stool; undercooked meat containing cysts or pseudocysts.
  • Blood transfusion during the acute stage and organ transplant in the chronic stage.

5. Clinical picture

GroupPicture
ImmunocompetentMost cases asymptomatic. A small percentage of acute cases show flu-like symptoms (fever, fatigue, muscle pain, sore throat, headache), cervical lymphadenopathy (the most common symptom), and atypical pneumonia. Self-limited, resolving within months.
ImmunocompromisedReactivation of latent infection (opportunistic). CNS impairment is the most common pathology in HIV: toxoplasmic encephalitis. Myocardium, lung and skeletal muscle may be affected.
Congenital1st trimester: abortion. 2nd trimester: stillbirth. 3rd trimester: hydrocephaly, intracerebral calcifications, neonatal jaundice, mental retardation, chorioretinitis. Transmission rate is highest in the third trimester but severity is greatest in the first. Most infants look normal at birth and, if untreated, develop the manifestations later.
OcularHallmark is necrotizing retinochoroiditis, primary or recurrent.

6. Diagnosis

Clinical symptoms are non-specific and not enough for a definite diagnosis.

Direct: detection of the organism in lymph nodes, tissues and body fluids. Microscopy in the acute stage: impression smears of lesions, dried 10-30 min, fixed in methyl alcohol and stained with Giemsa. Electron microscopy (tachyzoites always inside vacuoles), immunohistochemistry with labelled antisera, PCR for toxoplasma DNA, animal inoculation or tissue culture.

Indirect (serology):

TestKey points
Sabin-Feldman dye testThe standard reference test, sensitive and specific neutralisation test, measures IgG. Antibodies appear in 2-3 weeks and make the membrane of live cultured parasites impermeable to methylene blue, so parasites stay unstained. Unstained = positive (previous exposure). Disadvantages: high cost, hazard of live organisms, available mainly in reference laboratories.
IFA and ELISAMeasure IgM antibodies.
ResultMeaning
IgM raisedRecent infection in a person with normal immunity. IgM is undetectable after cure.
IgG positivePersists for years: past or chronic infection (more than 6 months).
Both negativeExcludes the diagnosis.
Both positivePossible infection within the past 12 months or a false-positive IgM; send to a reference laboratory.
In AIDSAntibody detection is unreliable: patients may not produce IgM and IgG may be low.

Congenital: in the mother, IgM and IgG serology. In the fetus, prenatal PCR on amniotic fluid (safer and more sensitive than fetal blood sampling) and serology of infant blood. In the newborn, specific IgM plus a rising IgG titre during the first year, or persistence of IgG at one year. Maternal IgG falls to undetectable between 6 and 12 months; the standard to rule out the diagnosis is IgG falling to zero by 12 months without treatment.

7. Treatment and prevention

Treat in four situations: pregnant women with acute infection, congenitally infected infants, immunosuppressed persons (usually reactivated disease), and acute or recurrent ocular disease. The drugs act mainly on the actively dividing tachyzoite and do not eradicate encysted bradyzoites.

  • Non-pregnant: pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine + folinic acid, or pyrimethamine + clindamycin + folinic acid.
  • Pregnant: spiramycin until delivery.

Prevention: wash hands, boards and sinks after handling meat; tissue cysts are killed by heating meat throughout to 67 °C or cooling to -13 °C; do not taste meat while cooking. Pregnant women avoid contact with cats, soil and raw meat. Feed pet cats only dry, canned or cooked food and empty the litter box daily. Wear gloves for gardening and wash vegetables. There is no vaccine for humans.

8. Cryptosporidium parvum

PointDetail
ClassAn intestinal coccidian, with Isospora belli and Cyclospora cayetanensis. All produce an oocyst (the infective stage), all are obligate intracellular, and all are opportunistic: harmless in immunocompetent hosts, severe in immunocompromised ones.
Hosts and habitatFound worldwide, especially the tropics. Infects many mammals, mainly new-born calves. The species in man is C. parvum. Both calves and man act as definitive and intermediate hosts. Habitat is the small intestine; the parasite is intracellular but extra-cytoplasmic. In immunocompromised hosts it may also be in lung, liver, gall bladder and pancreas.
Oocyst4-6 um, round, four sporozoites, thick wall, no sporocysts. It is the diagnostic and the infective stage. Thin-walled oocysts cause internal autoinfection; thick-walled oocysts are excreted and transmit to another host. Sporulation happens inside the body, so the oocyst is infective as soon as it is passed.
TransmissionSwallowing oocysts in food or water contaminated with calf faeces; internal and external autoinfection; water-borne outbreaks; accidental laboratory infection.
Life cycleExcystation in the small intestine; sporozoites parasitise epithelial cells and multiply asexually (schizogony), giving two types of merozoites. Type I reinvade epithelial cells and repeat the asexual cycle; type II start the sexual cycle, forming micro- and macrogametes. Fertilisation gives oocysts that sporulate in the host, thin-walled (autoinfection) and thick-walled (excreted).
ClinicalImmunocompetent: profuse watery diarrhoea with mucus but no blood, spontaneous cure in 1-3 weeks, from malabsorption, impaired digestion, fluid influx and parasite enterotoxins. Immunocompromised: prolonged and life-threatening, with extra-intestinal disease such as respiratory problems, pancreatitis, hepatitis and cholangitis.
DiagnosisDirect: oocysts in stool by modified Ziehl-Neelsen after concentration; immunofluorescence with monoclonal antibodies for coproantigen. Indirect: ELISA for stool coproantigen; serum antibodies show only past infection, but immunoblots for sporozoite antigens diagnose recent infection.
TreatmentNo drug eradicates the parasite completely. Nitazoxanide 200 mg twice daily for three days is the drug of choice, prolonged in immunosuppressed patients. Paromomycin has some effect. Add supportive care: antidiarrhoeals, fluids and electrolytes, hyper-immune serum and good nutrition in the immunosuppressed.
PreventionPersonal cleanliness, careful washing of fruit and vegetables, safe water supply, sanitary disposal of human faeces, and treating both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients to reduce transmission.

10. Numbers and traps worth memorising

Numbers: oocyst 11-14 x 9-11 um, two sporocysts of 4 · trophozoite 6 x 2 um · pseudocyst 50-100 tachyzoites · meat 67 °C or -13 °C · maternal IgG gone by 6-12 months · Crypto oocyst 4-6 um · Cyclospora 8-10 um · Isospora 30 x 12 um · nitazoxanide 200 mg twice daily for 3 days.
  • Transmission rate vs severity: highest transmission in the third trimester, greatest severity in the first. The exam loves swapping them.
  • Only cats shed oocysts. Human stool is not the diagnostic sample for toxoplasmosis; tissue cysts and pseudocysts are.
  • Dye test logic: antibodies protect the parasite membrane, so no dye enters, so unstained = positive.
  • IgM at birth comes from the infant; IgG at birth may be the mother, because IgG crosses the placenta.
  • Drugs hit tachyzoites only. Cysts stay, which is why latent infection can reactivate.
  • Crypto sporulates inside the body (infective on passage, autoinfection) while Cyclospora sporulates outside.
  • Crypto is extra-cytoplasmic, Cyclospora intra-cytoplasmic: the classic table row.
  • Modified Ziehl-Neelsen is the stain for coccidian oocysts; Giemsa is for the Toxoplasma smear.
