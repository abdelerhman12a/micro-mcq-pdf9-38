● During the parasite life cycle, sandflies transmit the promastigote of
Leishmania into humans through a skin bite for a blood meal. The
promastigotes invade the reticuloendothelial cells and transform into
amastigotes, which actively reproduce, causing tissue destruction. Reproduction
and invasion of additional cells then occur. The skin and mucous membranes of
the body are primarily affected. The diagnostic stage is the amastigote which
infects the sandfly during its blood meal from an infected human, the
amastigotes transform back into promastigotes in the fly midgut. These
promastigotes multiply and migrate into the salivary gland of the fly, where
they are ready to be transferred to a new human during a blood meal.

Clinical Classification of leishmaniasis:
(1) Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL):
Caused by Leishmania tropica; L. major & L. aethiopica.
(2) Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL):
Caused by Leishmania braziliensis & L. mexicana.
(3) Visceral leishmaniasis (VL):
Caused by Leishmania donovani & L. infantum.

I- Cutaneous leishmaniasis
A) Localized forms "Oriental sore":
– Lesion: nodule at the site of bite (on face or hand) that becomes
ulcerated; then heals spontaneously leaving scar.

Types:
1-Diffuse Cutaneous leishmaniasis (DCL)
- Caused by Leishmania aethiopica in immunosuppression.
- Mechanism of lesions: rapid multiplication & dissemination of parasites
(due to immunosuppression).
- Nodules are full of amastigotes; while intradermal test is negative (due to
immunosuppression).
2-Leishmania recidivans (LR)
– A recurrent form of leishmaniasis that occurs after healing of
Leishmania tropica infection in hypersensitive persons.
– It starts as small nodules at the edge of the previously healed lesion,
then spreads all over the body.
– Mechanism of lesions: Hypersensitivity to the remnants of
Leishmania tropica antigens in skin.
– Nodules are free of amastigotes; while intradermal test is strong
positive (due to hypersensitivity).
◙ NB; Both DCL & LR are rare & very difficult to cure.

Morphological types: In man T. cruzi occurs in 2 forms.

(A)Trypomastigote form (one shape only monomorphic).
* Present in blood only (extracellular).
* C or S shaped about 20 u in length.
*Large posterior kinetoplast with undulating membrane and free flagellum.
* Non-dividing form.

Trypomastigote form

(B) Amastigote form Leishmanoid form (intracellular).
* Present in tissues (RES- heart muscle).
* Actively dividing by simple binary fission.

Infective stage: Metacyclic form.
Mode of infection:
1- Contamination of bite wound by metacyclic forms in stool of Triatoma megista.
This infection is called posterior station i.e. the infective stage is present in the

Life cycle:

The ―kissing‖ bug (Triatoma) becomes infected by feeding on human or animal
blood that contains circulating parasites. The ingested trypomastigotes transform
into epimastigotes in the vector’s midgut. The parasites multiply, pass to hindgut
where they are differentiated into infective metacyclic trypomastigotes. An
385


=== PDF page 394 (printed 387) ===
infected Triatoma insect vector takes a blood meal and releases metacyclic
trypomastigotes in its faeces near the site of the bite wound. By scratching the site
of the bite, trypomastigotes enter the host through the wound, or through intact
mucosal membranes, such as the conjunctiva.

Pathogenesis:
- Inside the host, the trypomastigotes invade cells, where they differentiate
into intracellular amastigotes.

- The amastigotes multiply by binary fission and differentiate into
trypomastigotes, and then are released into the circulation as bloodstream
trypomastigotes.

- Trypomastigotes infect other cells and transform into intracellular
amastigotes in new infection sites.

- The blood stream trypomastigotes do not replicate (different from the
African trypanosomes).

- Replication resumes only when the parasites enter another cell or are
ingested by another vector.

- Clinical manifestations can result from the intracellular amastigotes which
destroy the intramural neurons of the autonomic nervous system in the
intestine and heart, leading to mega-intestine and heart aneurysms,
respectively.

Clinical picture:
There are two main stages of infection in Chagas’ disease; each stage has different
symptoms.
Acute stage:
In the acute phase, a local skin nodule called a Chagoma appears at the site of
inoculation. When the inoculation site is the conjunctival mucous membranes, the
patient may develop unilateral periorbital edema, conjunctivitis, and periauricular
lymphadenitis. This picture is called Romaña's sign. Sometimes a person
develops fatigue, fever, enlarged liver or spleen, lymphadenopathy and
myocarditis.
Chronic stage:
The symptomatic chronic stage may not occur for years or even decades after
initial infection. The disease affects the nervous system, digestive system and
heart. Chronic infections result in various neurological disorders, including
dementia, damage to the heart muscle (cardiomyopathy, the most serious
manifestation), and sometimes dilation of the digestive tract (megacolon and mega
esophagus).

Diagnosis:
1- For both acute and chronic Chagas’ disease, a history of possible exposure to
T. cruzi should be sought.
2- In acute Chagas’ disease, we depend mainly on direct methods
3- In chronic Chagas’ disease we depend mainly on indirect methods as the
parasite in blood is very scanty.

Direct methods of diagnosis:
1- Direct microscopic examination of anticoagulated blood or a buffy coat
preparation for motile Trypanosomes.
2- Thin or thick blood film stained with Giemsa for visualization of parasites.
3- Biopsy from lymph nodes or skeletal muscles to detect amastigotes
4- Culture of blood on NNN media.
5- Xenodiagnosis where uninfected winged bugs are fed on the patient's blood, and
their gut contents examined for parasites 4 weeks later.
Indirect methods of diagnosis:
1- Indirect hemagglutination
2- Complement fixation.
3- Immunoblot technique to detect antibodies.
4- PCR.
Treatment:
Medication for Chagas’ disease is usually effective when given during the early
acute stage of infection. The drugs of choice are benzonidazole or nifurtimox. In
the late chronic stages of infection, treatment focuses on managing the symptoms
associated with the disease.
Prevention and control:
●

Use insecticides to kill bugs and reduce the risk of transmission.

●

Remain aware that, in some countries, the blood supply may not always be
screened for Chagas’ disease and blood transfusions may carry a risk of
infection.
