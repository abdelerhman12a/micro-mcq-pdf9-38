Enterobacteriaceae

Fig 1.33: Gram negative bacilli

General features
Members of Enterobacteriaceae share features that help differentiating them
from other Gram-negative bacilli:
(1) They are facultative anaerobes
(2) They are oxidase negative
(3) They all ferment glucose (fermentation of other sugars is variable, lactose
fermentation is important differential character).
(4) They reduce nitrate to nitrite

Classification
• According to their medical importance, they are classified into:
1- The opportunistic pathogen: Most Enterobacteriaceae as Escherichia,
Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, Serratia & Proteus. Commensals and
may cause opportunistic infections.
2- The pathogenic genera: Salmonella, Shigella & Yersinia, & certain strains
of Escherichia coli. Primarily cause intestinal infections and systemic
diseases.
• According to their ability to ferment lactose, they are classified into:
1- The lactose fermenters (LF):
• Include Escherichia, Klebsiella, Citrobacter, Enterobacter, and Serratia
genera.

Antigenic structure
The antigens used to define groups and types of salmonellae include:
1. The O (somatic) antigens: which divide Salmonella into serogroups. The O
antigen may be shared between different groups.
2. The H (flagellar) antigens: which divide the serogroups into serotypes.
3. The Vi (capsular polysaccharide) antigen: which may be present in some
strains of S. Typhi.

Classification
• Clinically: Salmonella species are categorized into:
1. Typhoidal species: Salmonella Typhi (causing typhoid fever), S.
Paratyphi A, S. Paratyphi B and S. Paratyphi C (causing paratyphoid
fever).
2. Nontyphoidal species: S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium that cause
enterocolitis and S. Choleraesuis that causes septicaemia with metastatic
infections.

Typhoid and Paratyphoid Fever
(Enteric Fever)
• Enteric fever is a generalized infection of the reticuloendothelial system
and intestinal lymphoid tissue accompanied by bacteraemia and sustained
fever. It is strictly human disease.
• It is endemic in Egypt and caused by Salmonella typhi, and S. paratyphi
A, B, C. Typhoid fever is more common and tends to be more severe than
paratyphoid fever.
• Source of infection: cases or carrier who discharge the organism with their
faeces or urine.

Pathogenesis
• Humans are the only reservoir, either patients or healthy carriers.
• The infection is transmitted faeco-oral through contaminated food or drinks
e.g. raw vegetables, fruits, raw shellfish and milk products.
• After ingestion, the organisms adhere to the mucosa of the small intestine
(primary site of infection), then invade to the submucosal layer and taken up
by macrophages of the Peyer's patches where they replicate intracellularly.
• They are transported in the macrophages to the mesenteric lymph nodes and
via the thoracic duct to the bloodstream causing transient primary
bacteremia for 1-2 weeks (incubation period).
• The organisms reach organs and concentrate in reticuloendothelial system
(i.e. the spleen, bone marrow, liver and Peyer's patches).
• The organisms multiply in these organs and then reinvade the blood causing
secondary bacteraemia (onset of fever).
• From the blood, the organisms can reach other organs (e.g. kidney and gall
bladder).
• From the gall bladder, salmonellae enter the intestine for a second time in much
larger numbers and excreted with faeces (second week of the disease). In about
25% of cases, the organism is excreted in the urine.
• The patient may recover or become chronic carrier of Salmonella in the gall
bladder or in the urinary tract resulting in Intestinal carriers or Urinary
carriers

Clinical picture
• Incubation period: 1 – 2 weeks.
• Symptoms: fever, headache, malaise, tender abdomen,

constipation,

enlarged spleen and rose spots on the abdomen.
• Complications: include intestinal hemorrhage and perforation.

Laboratory diagnosis of enteric fever
Specimen: blood, stool, urine and serum depending on the stage of disease.
A. During the first week of fever:
1. Blood culture: 5 – 10 ml blood added to 50-100 ml blood culture bottle with
bile salt broth at 37ºC.
2. Bone marrow cultures: always positive.
3. Clot culture: blood is allowed to clot. Clot is used for culture and the serum is
separated for serological test.
Subculture is done on MacConkey or DCA medium, colonies are further
identified by:
- colony morphology
- Microscopic (wet film and Gram stain).
- Biochemical reactions.
- Slide agglutination test with specific antisera.
B. During the second week of fever and onwards:
1. Stools culture: stool sample is inoculated first in selenite broth or tetrathionate
broth overnight. Then subcultured on MacConkey or DCA agar.
Identification is done as mentioned before. Excretion of bacteria in the stool
may be intermittent. Repeated stool culture is needed.
2. Urine culture: urine is centrifuged. Deposit is cultured on MacConkey or DCA
medium. Identification is carried out as mentioned before.
3. Serologic diagnosis: can be done by Widal test and more sensitive methods as
ELISA and immunoblot assay.

Widal test: a tube agglutination test designed to detect anti-salmonella antibodies
titer in the patient’s serum.
Serial dilutions of the patient’s serum (1/20, 1/40, 1/80, 1/160….etc.) are
made in sets of test tubes; to each set, a suspension of O and H antigens of the
different salmonella, is added. The tubes are inoculated, then examined for
presence of agglutination with the different suspensions. The highest dilution of
the serum which gives agglutination is the end titer.
Interpretations of Widal test:
• For proper interpretation a rising titer is measured. It is a 4-fold rise of titer
in second serum samples collected one week after the first one.
• If only one serum sample is available, the following facts should be
considered:
a. The endemicity of the disease: in endemic areas, e. g., Egypt, high titer of O
and H ≥ 1/160 is indicative of recent infection. Titers ≤ 1/80 are not significant
due to repeated exposure to subclinical infection (community titer).
b. Past vaccinated or past infected individuals: high anti-H titer is the only
important, as O antibodies appear and disappear faster than H antibodies and
their presence is indicative of recent infection.
c. False positive results may be seen in some autoimmune diseases due to the
presence of cross-reacting antibodies.
d. False negative results may occur if the test is done very early in the first week
of illness or early antibiotic treatment lowers antibody titer due to reduction
of the antigenic mass.

C. Diagnosis of carriers: high titer of anti-Vi antibodies are detected in carriers
of Salmonella typhi.
1. Intestinal carriers are diagnosed by isolating the organism from the stool. The
patient is given first a cholagogue to empty the gall bladder followed by saline
purge.
2. Urinary carriers are diagnosed by isolating the organism from the urine.
Note that excretion of bacteria in stool and urine is intermittent, so repeated
examination is necessary.

Treatment
• The drug of choice is either fluoroquinolones (e.g. ciprofloxacin) or 3rd
generation cephalosporins (e.g. ceftriaxone).
• Ampicillin or ciprofloxacin should be used in chronic carriers of S. Typhi.

Prevention
A-Hygienic measures:
1- Proper sewage treatment and chlorinated water supply.
2- Hand wash prior to food handling.
3- Food handlers are examined to diagnose carriers, who should be treated or
excluded from handling food.
B-Immunological measures:
1- Oral typhoid vaccine: A live avirulent mutant strain of S. Typhi.
2- Vi capsular polysaccharide vaccine of S. Typhi: It is given intramuscularly.
3- TAB vaccine: A heat killed vaccine containing S. Typhi, S. Paratyphi A and
S. Paratyphi B. The vaccine is given subcutaneously. No longer
recommended, because of higher rate of side effects (pain and inflammation
at the site of the injection), except where the newer preparations are not
available.

Salmonella Food Poisoning
(Gastroenteritis or Enterocolitis)
It is intestinal infection (enteritis) with no blood invasion caused by Salmonella
typhimurium and Salmonella enteritidis.
• Source of infection: animals, or poultry.
• Mode of transmission: ingestion of

improperly cooked meat, eggs or

contaminated food.
• Incubation period: 12 – 48 hours (relatively long compared to other food
poisoning caused by preformed toxins).
• Clinical picture: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, severe diarrhea and mild
fever. It is self-limited and recovery occurs within one week.
• Laboratory diagnosis: isolation of the organism from the stools, vomitus and
food remnants. Blood culture and serological tests are of no value (no blood
invasion).
• Treatment: Fluid and electrolyte replacement may be needed. Antibiotics are
indicated only for neonates or persons with chronic diseases who are at risk of
septicemia and disseminated abscesses.

Shigella
Shigella species are strict human pathogens that cause bacillary dysentery in man.

Morphology:
• Non-motile, non-capsulated, Gram-negative bacilli.

Culture:
• Pale lactose non-fermenting colonies on MacConkey and DCA media.

Biochemical reactions
• Shigella dysenteriae: ferment glucose only, with production of acid only.
• Other Shigella species: ferment glucose and mannite with acid production
only.

Non-Fermentative Gram-Negative Bacilli

Characteristic features
1. Non-fermentative, motile, Gram-negative bacilli.
2. Aerobic.
3. Most species are oxidase positive.
Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the most important species.

Characteristic features
▪ P. aeruginosa is worldwide in distribution preferring moist environments:
▪ It is found in water, soil and on plants.
▪ It may be part of the normal microbial flora, particularly in the
gastrointestinal tract and moist body sites.
▪ It can grow in water containing only traces of nutrients, e.g. tap water.
▪ It also has a remarkable ability to resist disinfectants. These factors favour
its persistence in the hospital environment.

Morphology: Gram-negative motile bacilli
Cultural characters: It grows on:
• Nutrient agar: It usually produces diffusible exopigments that has bright
green color giving characteristic P. aeruginosa colonies.
• Blood agar: It causes complete haemolysis.
• MacConkey agar: It produces pale lactose non-fermenting colonies.
• TSI: It gives red (alkaline) butt and red (alkaline) slant.
• It has a grape-like (fruity) odour.

Biochemical activities
• P. aeruginosa is oxidase positive.
• It is carbohydrate non-fermenter
